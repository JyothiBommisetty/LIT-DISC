Ten days after the war ended, my sister Laura drove a car off a bridge. The bridge was being repaired: there were barrels and barricades and a detour sign, but she ignored them. Or perhaps she failed to see them, because the sun was in her eyes. Or perhaps she saw them but decided to drive through them anyway, because it was a fine day and there was a ravine at the bottom and she wanted to see what was there. Nobody knows, because nobody saw it happen. It was late at night, and there was no moon. The car was a Marion, a 1947, dark green, with fenders like outspread wings. It was the car our father had bought for her when she turned eighteen, and she had never bothered to change it. She was twenty-five when she drove it off the bridge. When they dragged the car out of the ravine, she was still strapped into the driver's seat, her hands on the wheel, her eyes wide open. She looked as if she had been expecting something, and was not surprised to find it.
