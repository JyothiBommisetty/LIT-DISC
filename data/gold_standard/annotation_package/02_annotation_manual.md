# LIT-DISC Annotation Manual

## A Detailed Guide to Scoring Literary Discourse Richness

---

### Using This Manual

For each of the 25 passages, you will assign a score on a 1-7 Likert scale for seven axes. This manual defines each axis, explains what high and low scores mean, and provides examples from canonical literary works.

**General principles:**

- Score the passage as it is, not what you imagine it could be
- A passage can be rich in some dimensions and poor in others — this is expected
- The "Overall LDRI" score is holistic: it integrates all six dimensions and considers how they interact
- There are no right or wrong answers; we seek your expert judgment
- If unsure, use the confidence scale honestly

---

### Axis 1: Semantic Richness (S)

**Definition.** The density, precision, and sophistication of lexical choices; the use of figurative language, wordplay, polysemy, connotation, and register variation.

**Literary meaning.** Semantic richness captures how language is used not merely to convey information but to create layers of meaning. A semantically rich passage rewards rereading: new connotations, ambiguities, or allusions emerge with each pass. This dimension is related to but distinct from vocabulary size — it concerns how words are deployed, not merely how many are used.

**Indicators of high Semantic richness (scores 6-7):**
- Unusual or precisely chosen vocabulary that cannot be replaced by a synonym without loss
- Dense metaphor, simile, or extended figurative language
- Polysemy: words carrying multiple simultaneous meanings
- Register shifts (e.g., sudden formality in a colloquial passage, or vice versa)
- Wordplay, puns, or etymological resonance
- Allusion or intertextuality embedded in word choice

**Indicators of low Semantic richness (scores 1-2):**
- Predominantly common, generic vocabulary
- Literal, denotative language with minimal figurative extension
- No noticeable register variation
- Sentences where synonyms could be freely substituted without changing effect
- Clichéd or formulaic phrasings

**Common mistakes:**
- Confusing semantic richness with passage length (shorter passages can be semantically dense)
- Confusing semantic richness with difficulty (Joyce is semantically rich; impenetrable academic prose is not necessarily so)
- Penalising dialogue (dialogue can be semantically rich through subtext and register)

**Examples:**
- High (7): Joyce, *The Dead* — "His soul swooned slowly as he heard the snow falling faintly through the universe and faintly falling, like the descent of their last end, upon all the living and the dead." Polysemy of "swooned" (faint, ecstasy, death), chiasmus ("falling faintly / faintly falling"), extended metaphor of snow as mortality, alliteration, rhythmic prose.
- Low (2): "He walked down the street and went into the shop. He bought a newspaper and a cup of coffee. Then he went home." Generic vocabulary, literal description, no figurative language, no register variation.

### Axis 2: Narrative Richness (N)

**Definition.** The complexity and craft of narrative structure: tension curve, pacing, scene construction, temporal manipulation, and plot dynamics.

**Literary meaning.** Narrative richness concerns how the story is told, not what happens. A narratively rich passage employs structural techniques that create engagement, suspense, surprise, or resonance. This includes manipulation of time (flashback, foreshadowing, ellipsis), variation in narrative distance, and deliberate pacing.

**Indicators of high Narrative richness (scores 6-7):**
- Deliberate pacing: variation between fast and slow passages
- Narrative tension: build-up, climax, release
- Structural surprises: twists, revelations, shifts in perspective
- Temporal manipulation: flashback, foreshadowing, non-linear ordering
- Scene construction: clear narrative units with beginnings, middles, and ends
- Narrative framing: stories within stories, change in narrator reliability

**Indicators of low Narrative richness (scores 1-2):**
- Chronological, linear narration with no temporal manipulation
- Uniform pacing throughout
- No narrative tension or release
- Simple scene construction (e.g., "this happened, then this happened")
- No framing devices

**Common mistakes:**
- Confusing narrative richness with event frequency (a passage with many events is not necessarily narratively rich)
- Confusing narrative richness with emotional impact (a sad story can be narratively simple)
- Assuming first-person narration is inherently richer than third-person

**Examples:**
- High (6): Borges, *The Garden of Forking Paths* — Non-linear time, labyrinth structure, frame narrative (spy story contains philosophical meditation), temporal paradox, multiple possible outcomes presented simultaneously.
- Low (2): "He woke up, ate breakfast, went to work, came home, ate dinner, and went to bed." Chronological, uniform pacing, no tension, no structural device.

### Axis 3: Interaction Richness (I)

**Definition.** The complexity, subtext, and dramatic dynamics of dialogue and social interaction between characters.

**Literary meaning.** Interaction richness captures how characters communicate — what is said, what is not said, and how the exchange reveals power, desire, deception, or intimacy. Rich interaction involves multiple layers: the literal meaning of words, the subtext beneath them, the power dynamics between speakers, and the evolution of the exchange over its course.

**Indicators of high Interaction richness (scores 6-7):**
- Subtext: characters saying one thing while meaning another
- Asymmetry: imbalance of knowledge, power, or intention between speakers
- Turn-taking dynamics: interruptions, pauses, overlaps that carry meaning
- Register variation within dialogue: shifts in formality, dialect, or tone
- Dialogue that advances characterisation or plot through what is implied, not stated
- Non-verbal communication embedded in speech (gesture, silence, tone)

**Indicators of low Interaction richness (scores 1-2):**
- Dialogue that only conveys information literally
- All speakers using identical registers and styles
- No power dynamics or tension between speakers
- Exchanges that could be summarised without loss
- Solitary passages with no interaction

**Common mistakes:**
- Confusing interaction richness with quantity of dialogue (a passage with one line of dialogue can be high if that line is layered)
- Confusing interaction richness with politeness (tense, rude exchanges can be rich; polite ones can also be rich through restraint)
- Forgetting that silence and absence of speech can carry meaning

**Examples:**
- High (7): Austen, *Pride and Prejudice* opening — Mr. and Mrs. Bennet's exchange about Mr. Bingley. Mrs. Bennet's effusive scheming, Mr. Bennet's laconic irony, the power dynamic of marital disagreement conducted through civility, subtext of financial anxiety masked as social enthusiasm.
- Low (2): " 'Hello,' said John. 'Hello,' said Mary. 'How are you?' 'Fine, thanks.' " Informational exchange, no subtext, no asymmetry, interchangeable speakers.

### Axis 4: Symbolic Richness (Sy)

**Definition.** The use of motifs, symbols, allegory, and recurring imagery to carry meaning beyond the literal narrative.

**Literary meaning.** Symbolic richness captures how objects, actions, settings, or patterns accumulate meaning across a text. A symbol is not merely an object mentioned but an object that carries thematic weight. This dimension includes explicit symbolism (a cross representing faith) and implicit pattern-making (recurring colours, weather, animals).

**Indicators of high Symbolic richness (scores 6-7):**
- Recurring imagery or motifs that gain meaning through repetition
- Objects or settings that carry clear thematic resonance
- Allegorical or archetypal patterns
- Symbols that operate on multiple levels (personal, cultural, universal)
- Symbolic systems that interact (e.g., weather + architecture + colour creating a unified symbolic language)
- Symbols that are ambiguous rather than fixed

**Indicators of low Symbolic richness (scores 1-2):**
- Literal description with no extra meaning
- Objects mentioned but not invested with significance
- No recurring patterns or motifs
- Symbolism that is explained rather than evoked

**Common mistakes:**
- Confusing symbolic richness with mention of symbolic objects (a cross on a wall is not symbolic unless it carries meaning in context)
- Over-reading: not every detail is a symbol. Score what is meaningfully present, not what could theoretically be interpreted
- Confusing symbolic richness with thematic richness (they are related but distinct; theme can be developed without symbols)

**Examples:**
- High (7): Joyce, *The Dead* — Snow as symbol of mortality, paralysis, connection, and erasure. It operates personally (Gabriel's epiphany), culturally (Irish paralysis), and universally (death). The snow descends "upon all the living and the dead" — merging categories.
- Low (1): A passage where rain is just rain, a house is just a house, and no object carries extra meaning.

### Axis 5: Evolution Richness (E)

**Definition.** The representation of change over time: character development, relationship arcs, insight shifts, and trajectory.

**Literary meaning.** Evolution richness captures whether and how characters or relationships change across the passage. This may be a micro-shift (a moment of realisation in a short story) or a macro-trajectory (compressed development in an excerpt). Evolution can be positive (growth, learning) or negative (decline, corruption) and can affect individuals, relationships, or communities.

**Indicators of high Evolution richness (scores 6-7):**
- Clear character arc within or across the passage
- Pivotal moments of insight, decision, or transformation
- Relationship trajectories that shift (estrangement, reconciliation, discovery)
- Change that is psychologically plausible and motivated
- Contrast between earlier and later states of the same character
- Multiple characters undergoing different trajectories simultaneously

**Indicators of low Evolution richness (scores 1-2):**
- Static characters who do not change
- No relationship arc
- Passage focused on description or exposition rather than development
- Characters who are flat types rather than developing individuals

**Common mistakes:**
- Confusing evolution with action (a character who does many things but does not change is not displaying evolution)
- Confusing evolution with emotion (a character who feels strongly but does not change is not evolving)
- Expecting explicit change (evolution can be subtle — a slight shift in perspective counts)

**Examples:**
- High (6): Carver, *Cathedral* — The narrator moves from prejudice and discomfort (about the blind man) to a moment of profound connection. The arc is visible even in a short excerpt: from "I was not enthusiastic about his visit" to the transcendent experience of drawing the cathedral together.
- Low (1): A static descriptive passage of a landscape, no characters, no change.

### Axis 6: Emotion Richness (Em)

**Definition.** The range, intensity, and complexity of affective experiences represented in the passage.

**Literary meaning.** Emotion richness captures not just what emotions are present but how they are presented — their nuance, their mixing, their trajectory. Rich emotion is rarely simple: it involves conflicting feelings, ambivalence, subtle shifts, and the interplay between what a character feels and what they acknowledge feeling.

**Indicators of high Emotion richness (scores 6-7):**
- Multiple emotions present simultaneously or in rapid succession
- Emotional complexity: mixed feelings, ambivalence, irony of feeling
- Emotional trajectory: build-up, peak, and resolution or transformation
- Valence shifts: movement between positive and negative affect
- Emotion communicated through indirection: gesture, silence, understatement
- Emotion that feels earned by the narrative context

**Indicators of low Emotion richness (scores 1-2):**
- Single, flat emotional register throughout
- Emotion stated but not evoked ("he was sad")
- Sentimentality: emotion that feels unearned or manipulative
- No emotional content (purely expository or technical passage)

**Common mistakes:**
- Confusing emotion richness with negativity (a happy passage can be as emotionally rich as a sad one)
- Confusing emotion richness with intensity (subtle, quiet emotion can be rich; loud, simplistic emotion can be poor)
- Penalising restraint (Hemingway's understated emotion is rich, not absent)

**Examples:**
- High (7): Joyce, *The Dead* — Gabriel's epiphany mixes embarrassment, desire, jealousy, pity, and finally a kind of transcendent acceptance. The emotions are layered, conflicting, and resolved through the snow imagery. This is not simple sadness but complex emotional integration.
- Low (1): "He was angry. He stayed angry for a long time. Then he was not angry anymore." Single emotion, stated not evoked, no complexity.

---

### Axis 7: Overall LDRI (O)

**Definition.** A holistic judgment of the passage's literary discourse richness, integrating all six dimensions and considering how they interact.

**Literary meaning.** This is not the average of the six dimension scores. It is an overall assessment of how the passage works as a literary discourse. A passage might be moderate on every dimension individually but achieve something remarkable through their interaction. Conversely, a passage might be very high on one dimension but low on others — the overall score should reflect your sense of the passage's total literary achievement.

**Guidelines:**
- Consider synergy between dimensions (e.g., high Semantic + high Symbolic often produce more than the sum of their parts)
- Consider whether the passage achieves something distinctive, memorable, or moving
- Consider the passage's effectiveness within its own terms (a minimalist story can be high overall if it does what it sets out to do with precision)
- The overall score should correlate roughly with the dimension scores but is not mechanically derived from them

**Indicators of high Overall LDRI (scores 6-7):**
- Multiple dimensions working together at high levels
- The passage feels dense, layered, and rewarding of rereading
- It achieves a distinctive literary effect (epiphany, catharsis, revelation, resonance)
- Craft is evident in every aspect of the writing

**Indicators of low Overall LDRI (scores 1-2):**
- Functional, informative, or purely expositional prose
- No dimension reaches moderate richness
- The passage feels flat, generic, or forgettable

**Common mistakes:**
- Averaging the six dimension scores (this is not required of the expert, though the computational LDRI does it differently)
- Letting one very high dimension inflate the overall score (consider the others)
- Letting personal taste interfere (you may dislike the genre; score what is there, not what you wish were there)

---

### Confidence Scale

After scoring all seven axes, rate your confidence on a 1-5 scale:

| Score | Label | Meaning |
|-------|-------|---------|
| 1 | Very low | Guessing; passage is difficult to judge |
| 2 | Low | Several scores could be plausible; uncertain |
| 3 | Moderate | Reasonably sure of most scores; some dimensions uncertain |
| 4 | High | Confident in the scoring pattern |
| 5 | Very high | Certain; passage clearly displays its richness profile |

High confidence scores are not "better" — they simply indicate passages where the features are clearest. Low confidence on a passage is valuable information for the research team.
